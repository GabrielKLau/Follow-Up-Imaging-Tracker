Seen on CT, missed by policy

A free screening is only the first step.

Federal law makes recommended breast and lung cancer screening free for most insured patients.1 But when a screening finds something that needs a closer look, the scans that come next often are not. Whether a patient pays for them depends largely on the state they live in.

The path from screening to diagnosis

A screening exam can flag something suspicious, but it cannot say whether that finding is cancer. Only the follow-up tests can.2

  1. 1 Screening Mammogram or low-dose CT (LDCT) Covered without cost-sharing under the ACA
  2. 2 Abnormal result The radiologist sees a finding and recommends more imaging
  3. 3 Follow-up imaging Diagnostic mammogram, ultrasound, MRI, follow-up CT, or PET/CT Deductibles, copays and prior authorization may apply
  4. 4 Biopsy Image-guided tissue sampling when imaging can't settle it Often not protected
  5. 5 Diagnosis A clear answer, and treatment if needed

Steps 3 and 4 are where patients can get stuck.

What is screening “purgatory”?

Picture a patient who goes in for a routine screening and gets a call back: something showed up, and the radiologist recommends another scan. The screening cost them nothing. The next scan may come with a deductible, a copay or coinsurance, or a wait for the insurer to approve it.3

That stretch between “we saw something” and “here's what it is” is screening purgatory. Out-of-pocket costs can discourage people from finishing the follow-up their radiologist recommended.3,4 When follow-up is delayed or never happens, the main benefit of screening, catching cancer early, is lost.

A patchwork of state laws

There is no current federal law that guarantees free follow-up imaging after an abnormal screening. Some states have stepped in with their own laws, and those laws differ a great deal. Some cover follow-up after breast screening but not lung. Some name the specific scans they cover; others don't. Few stop insurers from requiring prior authorization, and few extend protection to a biopsy.

So two patients with the same finding can face very different bills depending on where they live and what kind of plan they have.

Why lung cancer protections lag behind breast cancer protections

Protections for follow-up after lung cancer screening are far less common than for breast cancer. Part of the reason is history. Breast cancer has decades of organized advocacy behind it, which helped bring landmark laws such as the Mammography Quality Standards Act of 1992.5 Lung cancer screening with low-dose CT is much newer: the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force first recommended it in 2013, and Medicare began covering it in 2015.6,7 Lung cancer has also long carried a stigma tied to smoking, which can weaken public support and advocacy.8

The gap in lung protections is best read as a policy gap that hasn't been filled yet, not a sign that lung patients need follow-up any less.

How the score works ↓

A new way to compare state laws

How we score states: the Follow-up Imaging Policy Index

Saying a state “has a law” doesn't tell you much. One law might cover a single follow-up scan; another might cover every scan, block prior authorization and include a biopsy. The Follow-up Imaging Policy Index (FIPI) is a scoring system we built to capture that difference in one number from 0 to 10.

Breast and lung are scored separately, each out of 5, and added together. Points come only from what the law's text explicitly says, not from how insurers apply it in practice. A law only counts if it covers follow-up imaging with no patient cost-sharing for the plans the state regulates.

The four things FIPI looks for

Up to 1 point per cancer

1. Coverage and cost-sharing

Does a law require follow-up imaging to be covered with no deductible, copay or coinsurance?

Why it matters: This is the heart of screening purgatory. Even modest out-of-pocket costs can keep people from finishing the follow-up their radiologist recommended.3,4

Up to 2 points per cancer

2. Range of scans

Does the law name the scans a patient may actually need?

Why it matters: A law that says only “follow-up imaging” leaves room for an insurer to argue that a specific scan isn't included. We give points for covering imaging modalities that are clinically indicated for cancer detection.

Up to 1 point per cancer

3. Prior authorization

Does the law stop insurers from requiring approval before the scan?

Why it matters: A free scan still helps little if the patient waits days or weeks for an insurer to sign off. Those waits delay a diagnosis and prolong the worry of not knowing.

Up to 1 point per cancer

4. Biopsy

Does the protection extend to an image-guided biopsy with no cost-sharing?

Why it matters: When imaging can't settle the question, a biopsy is the step that gives the answer. Protection that stops before the biopsy leaves the last step of the path uncovered.

Reading a score

A higher score means fewer places where a patient can fall through the cracks between screening and diagnosis. A low score doesn't always mean a state has no law: it can mean the law covers the scan but leaves out prior authorization, a biopsy, or lung screening entirely. For advocates and legislators, the domains double as a checklist of what a strong bill includes.

RSNA 2026

FIPI is being presented at the annual meeting of the Radiological Society of North America (RSNA) in Chicago, one of the largest medical meetings in the world.

Radiologists are the ones who spot the finding and recommend the next scan, so they see firsthand when patients don't come back. Bringing FIPI to RSNA puts the policy gap in front of the physicians best placed to explain it to lawmakers, and invites more of them into advocacy.
